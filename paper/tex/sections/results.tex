\section*{Results}

\subsection*{A target-centered framework for using simulation labels in Tm prediction}

The target task was nanobody melting-temperature (Tm) regression from
amino-acid sequence. Computational labels were treated as auxiliary prediction
tasks, not as stand-ins for experimental Tm. Each model used the pretrained
protein sequence model ESM2 to turn a sequence into a learned representation
that a Tm head and an auxiliary head shared, so the auxiliary task could reshape
the representation while the final objective stayed anchored to experimental Tm
(Fig.~\ref{fig:learning_framework}).

We used the benchmark split as a deliberately low-data target setting, with 57
experimental Tm labels for training, 114 for validation, and 396 for held-out
testing. To keep the comparison fair across very different auxiliary labels,
each source was tuned independently: for every source and every encoder regime we
searched the model architecture, the auxiliary-head coupling, and the learning
rate, dropout, and weight decay, selecting on the experimental Tm validation set
alone. Only the selected configuration was then evaluated on the held-out Tm test
set, and improvements were measured as paired differences in absolute error on the
same test examples. This target-centered protocol guards against a trap specific
to simulation-to-experiment learning: an auxiliary task can be learnable and
physically sensible yet do nothing for the experimental phenotype. Under this
protocol the tuned Tm-only baselines were 7.23$^\circ$C MAE with a frozen encoder
and 6.55$^\circ$C MAE with a fine-tuned (hot) encoder.

\subsection*{Which computational labels transfer, and by how much}

We first asked which auxiliary labels lower held-out Tm error once each is tuned
to the same target endpoint (Fig.~\ref{fig:scaling}). The strongest label was the
FEP mutation free energy: it reached the best held-out accuracy overall
(6.40$^\circ$C MAE with a fine-tuned encoder) and improved over the matching
Tm-only baseline in both encoder regimes (hot $-0.15^\circ$C, 90\% CI $-0.30$ to
$-0.01$; frozen $-0.22^\circ$C, 90\% CI $-0.36$ to $-0.08$).

The second label that clearly transferred was new to this work: an MD
native-contact stability label computed on the \emph{same} 1MEL and 4IDL
mutation scans used for FEP. With a frozen encoder this label improved held-out
Tm by $-0.20^\circ$C (90\% CI $-0.34$ to $-0.05$), statistically
indistinguishable from FEP in the same regime (paired FEP-minus-MD difference
$-0.03^\circ$C, 90\% CI $-0.21$ to $+0.16$). This is a change from earlier
reports that an MD-derived stability label did not help; the difference, examined
next, is the data design rather than the physical quantity.

The remaining labels were weak or null under the same tuning. ThermoMPNN
stability scores gave only a small, non-significant frozen gain
($-0.14^\circ$C, 90\% CI crossing zero), and the Rosetta-family scores --- the
plain Rosetta mutation score and Rosetta scores assigned to ESM2-proposed or
random variant sets --- sat at the Tm-only baseline in the frozen regime
($+0.00$, $-0.01$, and $+0.08^\circ$C, respectively) and did not help in the hot
regime. Two controls separated label content from model capacity. Increasing the
ESM2 encoder from 8M to 35M or 650M parameters did not lower Tm-only error and did
not by itself reproduce the FEP gain (Fig.~\ref{fig:scaling}c), and the
frozen-versus-hot comparison (Fig.~\ref{fig:scaling}d) showed that FEP improved on
its baseline in both regimes whereas the MD native-contact label improved only
when the encoder was frozen. These two behaviors define the two axes examined in
Fig.~\ref{fig:simulation_comparison} and Fig.~\ref{fig:variant_sources}.

\subsection*{Data design: matching the sequence scan makes an MD label transfer}

The MD native-contact label uses the same physical observable in two different
data designs, so it isolates the effect of data design from the choice of
quantity. Computed over an earlier screen of diverse nanobodies, the label did not
improve Tm: its held-out error sat at or slightly above the corresponding Tm-only
reference (Fig.~\ref{fig:simulation_comparison}a, upper group). Computed instead
over the FEP-matched mutation scans --- the same 1MEL and 4IDL scaffolds, all of
constant length --- the identical observable improved Tm and matched FEP with a
frozen encoder (Fig.~\ref{fig:simulation_comparison}a, lower group). Because the
two groups come from different training series with different absolute baselines,
each is shown against its own Tm-only reference and the two are not cross-compared
numerically.

The difference is a data-design confound, not the label arithmetic
(Fig.~\ref{fig:simulation_comparison}b). The diverse screen spans sequence lengths
from 58 to 461 residues and carries a nonzero label--length correlation
($r = +0.13$), a length signal the auxiliary task can latch onto instead of the
stability signal; the matched scans are of constant length, so no such confound
exists. Referencing each mutant's native-contact value to its wild type (a
$\Delta Q$) versus using the raw value is immaterial here, because a constant
offset is absorbed by the min--max scaling applied to every label. With the
confound removed, the matched-scan label scales with the number of auxiliary
labels: its paired improvement grows from $+0.09^\circ$C at 20 labels to
$-0.20^\circ$C at 320 labels (frozen encoder; 90\% CI $-0.34$ to $-0.05$;
Fig.~\ref{fig:simulation_comparison}c). Matching the auxiliary source's sequence
design to the target neighborhood, not the specific dynamical quantity, is what
lets the MD label transfer.

\subsection*{Physical observable: free energy transfers deeper than native contacts}

With data design matched, the two all-atom labels still differed in how deeply
they transferred (Fig.~\ref{fig:variant_sources}). The FEP mutation free energy
improved Tm in both encoder regimes: it cleared its baseline whether the encoder
was held fixed or fine-tuned (Fig.~\ref{fig:variant_sources}a,b). The MD
native-contact label improved Tm only with a frozen encoder; once the encoder was
fine-tuned it gave no net gain and, at small label counts, degraded prediction
(paired $+0.47^\circ$C at 20 labels, 90\% CI $+0.29$ to $+0.65$), recovering only
to the baseline by 320 labels. Directly, FEP beat the MD label in the hot regime
($-0.18^\circ$C paired, 90\% CI $-0.36$ to $-0.01$) while tying it when frozen.
The natural reading is that the alchemical free energy carries enough information
about the mutation's effect on stability to beneficially reshape the sequence
encoder, whereas a native-contact persistence label can usefully condition a fixed
representation but cannot steer encoder fine-tuning toward the Tm phenotype
(Fig.~\ref{fig:variant_sources}c). The physical content of the label sets the
\emph{depth} of transfer.

The other structure-based labels sat with the weaker side of this picture. Under
matched tuning, the Rosetta-family scores did not robustly improve Tm in either
regime, and ThermoMPNN gave only a weak, non-significant frozen gain
(Fig.~\ref{fig:variant_sources}a,b). The two Rosetta-scored variant sets
(ESM2-proposed and random variants) are included only as ordinary comparators in
the source menu of Fig.~\ref{fig:scaling}b; under tuning the ESM2-proposed set did
not outperform the random set, so we make no claim about physics-scored sequence
proposals as a design loop.

Across every label we tested, the improvements were selective rather than
additive. Two axes organize the pattern: a matched data design decides whether a
simulation label transfers at all, and the physical observable decides how deep
that transfer goes. Under both, only the FEP mutation free energy (in both
regimes) and the FEP-matched MD native-contact label (with a frozen encoder)
robustly improved low-data nanobody Tm prediction.

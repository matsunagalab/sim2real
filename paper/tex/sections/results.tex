\section*{Results}

\subsection*{A target-centered framework for using simulation labels in Tm prediction}

We first built the evaluation framework. The target task was
nanobody Tm regression from amino-acid sequence, and computational labels were
treated as source prediction tasks, not as stand-ins for experimental Tm. Each
model used the pretrained protein sequence model ESM2 to turn a sequence into a
learned representation, which a Tm head and a computed-label head shared. The
source task could therefore reshape the representation while the final objective
stayed anchored to experimental Tm (Fig.~\ref{fig:learning_framework}).

Every candidate setting was selected on an experimental Tm validation set, and
the final numbers came only from a held-out experimental Tm test set;
improvements were measured as paired differences in absolute error on the same
test examples. This guards against a trap specific to simulation-to-experiment
learning: a computational task can be learnable and physically sensible yet do
nothing for the experimental phenotype.

\subsection*{Scaling depends on the physical label}

We next varied the number of experimental and computational labels to see whether
more source data meant better Tm prediction. Adding experimental Tm labels helped
the Tm-only model, as expected (Fig.~\ref{fig:scaling}). The target-label sweep
used 10, 20, 30, 40, and 57 experimental Tm training labels; the FEP sweep used
10, 40, 80, 160, and 320 rows per template-specific source table; and the MD
Q-value sweep used 10, 40, 80, 160, 320, and 640 rows from the single MD source
table. FEP mutation free-energy labels showed a beneficial trend: the largest
FEP setting gave the best held-out Tm error among the FEP points. The curve was
not strictly monotonic, so we do not read it as a clean power law. The consistent
reading is that more mutation free-energy labels help when models are selected
and judged through the experimental Tm endpoint.

The MD-derived Q-value behaved differently. This label measures how well native
contacts persist during high-temperature MD trajectories. Adding more of these
labels did not reliably beat learning from Tm measurements alone, even though
they too came from simulation. The sweep thus sets up the central comparison
below: label count alone is not enough; what matters is the transferable physical
content of the label.

\subsection*{All-atom simulation labels differ in their transfer to Tm prediction}

We then focused on two all-atom source labels. Training on experimental Tm alone
reached a held-out test MAE of 6.61$^\circ$C. Adding FEP mutation free-energy
labels cut this to 6.26$^\circ$C, a consistent paired $\Delta$MAE of
-0.35$^\circ$C (90\% confidence interval, -0.46 to -0.24$^\circ$C;
Fig.~\ref{fig:simulation_comparison}); negative paired changes mean lower error
than the Tm-only reference. The MD-derived Q-value went the other way, raising
the test error to 6.73$^\circ$C (paired change, +0.12$^\circ$C; 90\% confidence
interval, +0.01 to +0.22$^\circ$C). Both labels came from atomistic simulation,
yet only the mutation free energy improved the experimental endpoint.

The encoder controls reinforced this split. With a frozen encoder, errors rose
overall, but FEP still beat the frozen Tm-only baseline: 7.20$^\circ$C versus
7.38$^\circ$C MAE. The gain was smaller than with a hot encoder, and the frozen
FEP model stayed worse than the hot-encoder FEP model. The MD Q-value helped in
neither regime. FEP labels therefore carry useful information even when the
representation is fixed, but adapting the encoder is what extracts the largest
target-task gain.

The failure of a terminal Q-value did not mean that all MD-derived descriptors
were equivalent. We therefore carried selected descriptor controls into the
same residual-control architecture and evaluated them on the held-out Tm test
set. Q-value slope was slightly better than the raw Q-value but did not reach
the FEP reference. Disulfide-distance fluctuation and CDR3 length gave the
lowest errors among these controls, indicating that the useful signal depends
on how the simulation or nanobody-specific descriptor is converted into a
source label.

\subsection*{The FEP gain is not explained by sequence-model size alone}

Because a protein language model's size can drive downstream performance, we
checked whether a larger encoder alone could reproduce the FEP benefit. It could
not. In the Tm-only setting, the 35M and 650M encoders did not beat the 8M
encoder on held-out performance. In the FEP setting, the 35M and 650M encoders
reached 6.50 and 6.65$^\circ$C MAE, both worse than the 8M FEP model. FEP labels
stayed beneficial within each size comparison, but the best absolute performance
came from the smallest encoder paired with FEP labels. Within the tested range,
the gain tracked the physical source label, not the scale of the language model
(Supplementary Fig.~4).

\subsection*{Structure-based labels provide weaker source signals}

We next looked at computational labels outside the FEP and MD pipelines. Rosetta
scores on ESM2-proposed variants reached 6.45$^\circ$C MAE (paired change,
-0.17$^\circ$C; 90\% confidence interval, -0.31 to -0.03$^\circ$C), and
ThermoMPNN stability scores reached 6.46$^\circ$C MAE (paired change,
-0.15$^\circ$C; 90\% confidence interval, -0.30 to -0.01$^\circ$C;
Fig.~\ref{fig:variant_sources}). Rosetta mutation scores reached 6.53$^\circ$C
MAE (paired change, -0.09$^\circ$C; 90\% confidence interval, -0.20 to
+0.02$^\circ$C), and random variants scored by Rosetta reached 6.51$^\circ$C MAE
(paired change, -0.10$^\circ$C; 90\% confidence interval, -0.22 to
+0.03$^\circ$C). All were weaker than FEP, and the two Rosetta controls had
confidence intervals that crossed zero.

The ESM2-proposed variants scored by Rosetta edged out the random variants, with
a paired MAE difference of -0.07$^\circ$C in favor of the ESM2-proposed set. The
confidence interval crossed zero, so we read this as exploratory rather than as
evidence that the present generator improves variant selection. It does show,
though, that language-model proposals can be annotated by physics-based scoring
and used as source labels for the Tm predictor---a feasibility point we return to
in the Discussion.

Across the labels we tested, the strongest and most consistent improvement came
from FEP mutation free energies. Other structure-based labels and proposal-set
scores gave weaker gains, and the MD-derived Q-value gave none. The pattern is
selective, not additive: a computational label helps only when the information it
carries transfers to the experimental Tm endpoint.

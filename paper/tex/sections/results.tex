\section{Results and discussion}

\subsection{The prediction target and the Tm-only baseline}

Every model predicted experimental Tm for the same 396 held-out sequences, and
every model's architecture, hyperparameters, and checkpoint were chosen on the Tm
validation set (Fig.~\ref{fig:learning_framework}); the computed labels entered
only as auxiliary training targets. We report each computed-label model against a
baseline trained on Tm alone under matched conditions (the same regression
architecture family, encoder setting, and training budget), the Tm-only model, so
that a change in MAE ($\Delta$MAE) below zero means the computed labels lowered the
Tm error.
We ran two comparisons that were tuned separately, so each has its own Tm-only
baseline. In the comparison among labels computed on the 1MEL and 4IDL
structures, the Tm-only baseline reached an MAE of $7.23^\circ$C with frozen ESM2
and $6.55^\circ$C with fine-tuned ESM2. The heterogeneous MD study used a separate
validation search, with a different sample budget and more training seeds, and so
had a different Tm-only baseline ($7.38$ and $6.61^\circ$C). We therefore
compared each computed-label model only with the Tm-only baseline from its own
comparison and encoder setting.

\subsection{The single mutation scan reduced Tm error more than a heterogeneous set}

We first varied $n$, the number of computed labels sampled per structure, for
the single mutation scan label (Fig.~\ref{fig:data_design}a). With a frozen encoder,
$\Delta$MAE moved from $+0.09^\circ$C at $n=20$ to $-0.20^\circ$C at $n=320$,
where its 95\% interval lay below zero. With a fine-tuned encoder it began well
above the baseline ($+0.47^\circ$C at $n=20$; 95\% CI, $+0.26$ to
$+0.69^\circ$C) and improved as $n$ grew, reaching an interval that included zero
by $n=160$. In both encoders $\Delta$MAE fell steadily as $n$ increased. Four
label counts are too few to fit a scaling exponent, but this monotonic
improvement is the qualitative data-scaling behaviour reported when computed data
are added to a smaller experimental set in Sim2Real materials-property prediction
\citep{Minami2025Scaling}.

We then compared two ways of building the MD label set
(Fig.~\ref{fig:data_design}b). The heterogeneous data set contained 1143 samples
representing 833 unique sequences of different lengths, whereas the single
mutation scan data set covered mutations of the fixed 1MEL and 4IDL structures.
Both used the same 400~K MD simulations and the same native-contact
$Q$ definition, with the same contact set and averaging window, so they differed
only in the variants they covered. Because the models for the two studies were
tuned separately, we compared each against its own Tm-only baseline. At the
largest label count (640 auxiliary samples per model: $n=320$ per structure for
the single mutation scan and $n=640$ for the heterogeneous set), adding the
MD label changed MAE by $-0.049^\circ$C with a frozen encoder (95\% CI,
$-0.092$ to $-0.006^\circ$C) and by $+0.116^\circ$C with a fine-tuned encoder
(95\% CI, $-0.009$ to $+0.243^\circ$C) in the heterogeneous study, and by
$-0.195^\circ$C (95\% CI, $-0.366$ to $-0.030^\circ$C) and $+0.029^\circ$C
(95\% CI, $-0.139$ to $+0.197^\circ$C) in the single mutation scan study. The
frozen-encoder reduction was thus larger for the single mutation scans.

Eight sequences in the heterogeneous MD data set also occurred in the Tm test
set, but excluding their test errors barely changed either estimate (frozen
$-0.049^\circ$C, 95\% CI $-0.094$ to $-0.005^\circ$C; fine-tuned
$+0.122^\circ$C, 95\% CI $-0.005$ to $+0.248^\circ$C). We did not retrain after
removing the overlapping samples, so this check addresses their contribution to
the test MAE but not a possible effect during training or model selection.

Because the two data sets shared one $Q$ definition, their different effect on
the Tm error must come from the variants each covered. A single mutation scan
holds the structural background fixed, so each label (through $\Delta Q$)
isolates the local effect of a single point mutation, the very kind of stability
change the model must learn for Tm. The heterogeneous set, by contrast, spans
many different nanobodies, so its $Q$ reflects whole-protein differences such as
length and fold rather than the effect of a single mutation. A label tied to
protein identity in this way carries only a loose
connection to Tm: it offers the shared representation little useful signal and can
bias it toward features that do not generalize to the target. Such a mismatch
between the auxiliary and target tasks is a known source of negative transfer, in
which a poorly matched auxiliary task fails to help or even degrades performance
\citep{Zhang2023NegativeTransfer}.

\subsection{FEP had the lowest test MAE with both encoders}

We then compared the labels by the physical quantity they report, each with a
model tuned separately on the Tm validation set
(Fig.~\ref{fig:physical_observable}). FEP, MD native-contact $Q$, Rosetta, and
ThermoMPNN were all computed on the same single mutation scan of 1MEL and 4IDL,
that is the same single point mutations, so these four labels differ only in the
physical quantity; the two remaining Rosetta labels scored two-point-mutation variants
of the same structures. With a frozen encoder, FEP reached an MAE of
7.01$^\circ$C, a $\Delta$MAE of $-0.221^\circ$C from the Tm-only baseline
(95\% CI, $-0.393$ to $-0.051^\circ$C). The single mutation scan reached an MAE of
7.03$^\circ$C ($\Delta$MAE $-0.195^\circ$C; 95\% CI, $-0.366$ to $-0.030^\circ$C),
and both intervals were below zero. The Rosetta labels were close to or worse than
the Tm-only baseline, and ThermoMPNN reached an MAE of 7.09$^\circ$C with an
interval that included zero. With a fine-tuned encoder, FEP reached an MAE of
6.40$^\circ$C, the lowest in the study; its $\Delta$MAE from the 6.55$^\circ$C
Tm-only baseline was
$-0.153^\circ$C (95\% CI, $-0.323$ to $+0.020^\circ$C), an interval that included
zero. The single mutation scan, Rosetta, ThermoMPNN, and random variants scored with
Rosetta differed from Tm-only by $+0.029$ to $+0.144^\circ$C, with intervals
that also included zero. ESM2-proposed variants scored with Rosetta increased
error by $+0.411^\circ$C (95\% CI, $+0.140$ to $+0.698^\circ$C).

Why should FEP $\Delta\Delta G$ transfer best? Of the quantities we tested, it is
the most direct thermodynamic proxy for what Tm reports: both track the
free-energy cost of losing the folded state, so a mutation that FEP scores as
destabilizing is, on average, the kind of mutation that also lowers Tm. The
high-temperature $Q$ instead measures contact retention over a finite trajectory,
and the Rosetta and ThermoMPNN scores approximate $\Delta\Delta G$ through an
empirical energy function or a learned structural model; each is one step further
from the measured transition. We would therefore expect $\Delta\Delta G$ to
reinforce the stability-related sequence features an encoder needs for Tm most
strongly, which is consistent with FEP giving the largest fine-tuned gain. This
interpretation is speculative: in earlier work we used sparse autoencoders to show
that ESM2 fine-tuned on nanobody Tm develops features for surface charge,
hydrophobic-core packing, the VHH tetrad, and disulfide bonds
\citep{Murakami2025Mechanistic}, but we did not analyse the representations learned
here, and comparing Tm-only, Tm+FEP, and Tm+MD models under one architecture would
be needed to test the mechanism directly.

\subsection{Robustness checks}

We ran several checks on the main comparison. First, to confirm that the FEP
benefit was not specific to the small 8M encoder, we repeated it with the 35M-
and 650M-parameter ESM2 models. Without size-specific tuning, FEP reduced MAE
relative to Tm-only for both, with both paired 95\% intervals below zero
(Supplementary Fig.~S1). The gain thus persisted across more than an order of
magnitude in encoder size without re-tuning, consistent with our earlier report
that nanobody Tm prediction is largely insensitive to ESM2 size
\citep{Murakami2025Mechanistic}.

Second, we asked whether proposing variants with ESM2, rather than at random,
before Rosetta scoring improved transfer. It did not: under our Rosetta protocol,
the ESM2-proposed variants gave no lower Tm error than variants drawn at random
(Fig.~\ref{fig:physical_observable}), so selecting variants by a language-model
score alone did not help.

Third, data-set size did not explain the contrast between the two MD studies. The
heterogeneous data set contained 1143 samples and the single mutation scan data
set 844, but each ensemble member sampled at most 640 auxiliary samples in both
studies, so the larger frozen-encoder reduction for the single mutation scans
cannot be attributed to more training labels per model. FEP and the single
mutation scan required similar simulation time per variant, although wall-clock
costs were not recorded consistently enough for a general cost comparison.

\subsection{Limitations and future directions}

Our study has several broader limitations. It rests on one small Tm data set (57
training nanobodies from a single benchmark), and the matched calculations cover
only two structures, so we cannot yet say how far the FEP advantage extends to
other nanobody families or to other measured stability properties. The two
comparisons were also tuned separately and differ in model details and variant
composition, so the ranking of computed quantities should be read as indicative
rather than definitive. The bootstrap intervals, which resample the fixed test
proteins after predictions are averaged across models, likewise omit the
uncertainty from selecting auxiliary samples, seeds, and settings. A stricter
test would need larger and more diverse Tm data, matched calculations on more
structures, and a single shared model across conditions; we also evaluated only
on existing measurements rather than new ones.

A natural direction is to make the calculations adaptive. Instead of fixing the
variants and the quantity in advance, one could extend the single mutation scans
to more structures and choose each new round of simulations where it is most
likely to help the Tm model, using active learning or reinforcement learning for
sequence design \citep{Angermueller2020RLSequence}. Such a loop would need to
outperform random and simpler active-learning baselines, and, because
ESM2-proposed variants did not beat random ones here, should not rely on a
computed score alone to decide what to simulate. Any gains should ultimately be
confirmed with new experimental Tm measurements.

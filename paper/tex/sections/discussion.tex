\section*{Discussion}

\subsection*{Source-label transfer is selective}

We asked how simulation-derived labels should be used when the experimental
target is scarce and the simulated quantity is related to, but not the same as,
that target. In nanobody Tm prediction, FEP mutation free-energy labels gave a
modest but consistent improvement in held-out Tm prediction, while an MD-derived
Q-value did not, and a larger ESM2 encoder did not reproduce the FEP gain either.
The picture is selective: simulation data help when the source label encodes
physical information that transfers to the experimental phenotype under
target-centered validation.

\subsection*{Why mutation free energies transfer}

The label that helped did not measure Tm. FEP estimates how a mutation changes
free energy, whereas Tm is the temperature at which the folded and unfolded
states balance under a given assay. These are not interchangeable quantities. Yet
they describe the same stability landscape from different angles: sparse Tm
measurements pin absolute stability for the sequences we observe, and mutation
free energies supply local directions in sequence space. The FEP result suggests
that a source label need not be a direct surrogate for the target, as long as it
describes a physically relevant perturbation of the target property.

\subsection*{What the controls rule out}

Three controls sharpen this reading. First, the FEP label-count sweep was
consistent with a benefit at the largest setting but was not smooth enough to
claim a biomolecular scaling law; the firmer conclusion is source dependence.
This both echoes and complicates the materials-informatics case, where the
transferred error scales with the size of a related computational database
\citep{Minami2025Scaling}: in our protein setting, the useful labels were not
extra Tm measurements but mutation free energies. Second, model size was not the
lever. The 35M and 650M ESM2 encoders did not beat the 8M encoder in either the
Tm-only or FEP settings, and the best model paired the 8M encoder with FEP labels,
in line with the broader finding that fine-tuning rather than raw capacity drives
PLM-based protein-property prediction \citep{Schmirler2024FineTuning}. Third, the
frozen-encoder experiment locates the signal: FEP labels helped even with a fixed
representation, but the best FEP model required updating the encoder. The labels
work best when allowed to reshape the representation toward stability-relevant
directions while still being selected on experimental Tm, which favors controlled
encoder adaptation over either frozen feature extraction or unconstrained
source-task optimization.

The MD Q-value result calls for the same specificity. It is not evidence that
MD-derived descriptors are generally unhelpful; this particular high-temperature
native-contact summary simply did not transfer under our protocol. By compressing
each trajectory to a single native-contact persistence statistic, it may miss the
mutation-specific energetics that govern how sequence changes shift stability. A
simulation campaign aimed at target transfer should choose descriptors for their
expected physical relevance to the endpoint, not for how many labels they yield.

\subsection*{Physics-scored sequence proposals as a future direction}

The Rosetta-scored proposal-set result is best read as a feasibility test for
design workflows. Rosetta scores on ESM2-proposed variants improved Tm prediction
over Tm-only learning, but the direct comparison with random variants stayed
within noise, so we do not claim that the present generator has already optimized
nanobody thermostability. The narrower, solid point is that language-model
proposals can be annotated by a physics-based score and used as supervised source
data for a Tm predictor.

That supervised-scoring step is the only part of a design loop we tested. A full
loop would let a generator propose variants, physics-based calculations annotate
them, the Tm predictor update its estimate of experimental stability, and that
estimate drive the next round of proposals---connecting this transfer-learning
result to active or reinforcement learning. Those closed-loop steps, and
experimental validation of newly proposed high-Tm nanobodies, are beyond the
present scope.

\subsection*{Limitations}

Several limitations bound the interpretation. The experimental Tm training set is
small, so the conclusions should be retested on other nanobody collections and
protein families. The source-label comparison covers only the labels available
here; other free-energy protocols, stability predictors, or trajectory
descriptors may transfer differently. The FEP curve supports a beneficial trend
at the largest setting but does not establish a clean scaling exponent. No new
experimental measurements were made to validate high-stability candidates. And
because raw trajectories may be too large to deposit in full, reproducibility will
rely on processed data, scripts, and a clearly reported simulation protocol.

\subsection*{Outlook}

For low-data experimental protein-property prediction, simulation labels are best
treated as design choices rather than generic data augmentation. The question is
not whether simulation can produce many labels, but whether those labels encode
information that improves the target task under target-centered validation. Here,
mutation free energies did that for nanobody Tm. The general recipe follows: use
experimental data to define the target phenotype, use simulation to annotate
physically meaningful perturbations around it, and judge the combined model only
on held-out experimental data. That is a practical route from simulation-assisted
prediction toward closed-loop protein engineering.

\section*{Discussion}

\subsection*{Source-label transfer is selective}

This study set out to clarify how simulation-derived labels should be used when
the experimental target is scarce and the simulated quantity is related to, but
not the same as, that target. In nanobody Tm prediction, FEP mutation free-energy labels gave a
modest but consistent improvement in held-out Tm prediction, while an MD-derived
Q-value did not, and a larger ESM2 encoder did not reproduce the FEP gain either.
The picture is selective: simulation data help when the source label encodes
physical information that transfers to the experimental phenotype under
target-centered validation.

\subsection*{Why mutation free energies transfer}

The label that helped did not measure Tm, and it is not normally used to predict
it. FEP estimates how a mutation changes free energy---a relative, per-mutation
$\Delta\Delta G$---whereas Tm is the absolute temperature at which the folded and
unfolded states balance under a given assay. The two are routinely handled as
separate prediction problems, with their own datasets and benchmarks, and a
relative free energy is not interchangeable with an absolute melting temperature.
They are not unrelated, though: both read out the same underlying stability
landscape, with sparse Tm measurements pinning absolute stability for the
sequences we observe and mutation free energies supplying local directions in
sequence space. The result here is that this shared physical content is strong
enough to cross the task boundary---$\Delta\Delta G$ supervision, selected only on
Tm, improves Tm prediction---even though the source labels were computed on
different template variants than the Tm test sequences.

Two features keep this from being a generic auxiliary-task effect. First, the
transfer is selective: an MD-derived Q-value, equally simulation-derived, does
not help, so the gain tracks the specific physical relatedness of $\Delta\Delta G$
to Tm rather than the mere presence of an extra task. Second, the direction is
practically useful. Tm is scarce, but relative stability data are not:
physics-based $\Delta\Delta G$ can be generated in bulk, and experimental
folding-stability measurements now exist at the scale of hundreds of thousands of
domains \citep{Tsuboyama2023MegaScale}. A working $\Delta\Delta G$-to-Tm bridge is
a route to put those abundant resources to work on the phenotype that remains hard
to measure.

\subsection*{What the controls rule out}

Three controls sharpen this reading. First, the FEP label-count sweep was
consistent with a benefit at the largest setting but was not smooth enough to
claim a biomolecular scaling law; the firmer conclusion is source dependence.
This both echoes and complicates the materials-informatics case, where the
transferred error scales with the size of a related computational database
\citep{Minami2025Scaling}: in our protein setting, the useful labels were not
extra Tm measurements but mutation free energies. Second, model size was not the
lever. The 35M and 650M ESM2 encoders did not beat the 8M encoder in either the
Tm-only or FEP settings, and the best model paired the 8M encoder with FEP labels,
in line with the broader finding that fine-tuning rather than raw capacity drives
PLM-based protein-property prediction \citep{Schmirler2024FineTuning}. Third, the
frozen-encoder experiment locates the signal: FEP labels helped even with a fixed
representation, but the best FEP model required updating the encoder. The labels
work best when allowed to reshape the representation toward stability-relevant
directions while still being selected on experimental Tm, which favors controlled
encoder adaptation over either frozen feature extraction or unconstrained
source-task optimization.

The MD Q-value result calls for the same specificity. It is not evidence that
MD-derived descriptors are generally unhelpful; this particular high-temperature
native-contact summary simply did not transfer under our protocol. By compressing
each trajectory to a single native-contact persistence statistic, it may miss the
mutation-specific energetics that govern how sequence changes shift stability. A
simulation campaign aimed at target transfer should choose descriptors for their
expected physical relevance to the endpoint, not for how many labels they yield.

\subsection*{Physics-scored sequence proposals as a future direction}

The Rosetta-scored proposal-set result is best read as a feasibility test for
design workflows. Rosetta scores on ESM2-proposed variants improved Tm prediction
over Tm-only learning, but the direct comparison with random variants stayed
within noise, so we do not claim that the present generator has already optimized
nanobody thermostability. The narrower, solid point is that language-model
proposals can be annotated by a physics-based score and used as supervised source
data for a Tm predictor.

That supervised-scoring step is the only part of a design loop we tested. A full
loop would let a generator propose variants, physics-based calculations annotate
them, the Tm predictor update its estimate of experimental stability, and that
estimate drive the next round of proposals---connecting this transfer-learning
result to active or reinforcement learning. Those closed-loop steps, and
experimental validation of newly proposed high-Tm nanobodies, are beyond the
present scope.

\subsection*{Implications for large-scale data collection}

The most useful output of this study is not the size of the improvement, which
was modest, but a rule for where to spend data-collection effort. Under
target-centered evaluation, a source label helped only when its physical quantity
was close to the target ($\Delta\Delta G$), and not when it was merely
simulation-derived (the MD Q-value) or merely backed by a larger model. The
practical reading is that a data campaign aimed at a scarce phenotype like Tm
should be chosen for physical relatedness to that phenotype, and then validated by
held-out target performance, before committing to scale.

For nanobody Tm specifically, three priorities follow. First, relative stability
at scale for VHH-length scaffolds. The strongest source here, $\Delta\Delta G$, is
the quantity that high-throughput proteolysis can already measure for on the order
of $10^5$ domains \citep{Tsuboyama2023MegaScale}, but those domains are short
(40--72 residues) whereas VHH domains are longer; extending such assays, or
computational $\Delta\Delta G$, to VHH-length scaffolds would supply the large,
physically matched source pool that our two-template FEP set only begins to
sample. Second, template and mutation diversity. Our FEP labels came from two
templates, enough to show transfer but not to scale it; $\Delta\Delta G$ spanning
many templates and mutations, broad enough to cover the sequence neighborhood of
the Tm target, is the natural next dataset. Third, modest but well-distributed Tm
anchors. The target side here was 57 sequences, and a few hundred Tm measurements
spread across the relevant stability range would fix the absolute scale that
$\Delta\Delta G$, being relative, cannot set on its own.

These choices also trade off against cost, which our results bracket even if we
did not measure it directly. The cheaper sources scale fastest: a learned
predictor such as ThermoMPNN labels a variant almost for free, and Rosetta scoring
costs minutes to hours of CPU, so either can build very large source pools
cheaply. Both transferred---less than FEP, but measurably---which points to a
pragmatic, cheap-and-many route when a modest gain is enough. The risk is
saturation: a proxy can carry the model only as far as its own accuracy allows, so
adding more cheap labels may stop helping once that ceiling is reached. All-atom
alchemical FEP sits at the other extreme---hours of GPU time per mutation, but the
strongest per-label transfer we observed. When the goal is to drive held-out Tm
error as low as possible, and to push that saturation point further out, spending
the extra cost on physically faithful $\Delta\Delta G$ is the better investment.
The implied strategy is tiered: use inexpensive learned or structure-based labels
to scale breadth, and reserve all-atom calculations for the sequence regions, or
the accuracy regimes, where they pay for themselves.

The negative results are equally prescriptive. Collecting more coarse trajectory
summaries, such as a single native-contact Q-value, is unlikely to repay the
simulation cost; if molecular dynamics is used as a source, the observable should
be designed to report mutation-specific energetics rather than a global stability
proxy. Enlarging the language model is likewise not a substitute for physically
matched labels.

These costs become interpretable once they are weighed against experimental
value. Minami et al. quantify that value as an equivalent sample size---the number
of simulation labels worth one experimental label, read off as the marginal rate
of substitution between the two along an equal-error contour
\citep{Minami2025Scaling}. Our experimental-label scaling reference exists to make
the same estimate here. Near the operating point of the study (57 Tm labels and
320 FEP labels per template), one experimental Tm label lowers held-out error by
about 0.009$^\circ$C, and matching that with FEP labels takes on the order of 30
per template, roughly 60 in total; the MD-derived Q-value, whose error does not
fall as labels are added, has an equivalent sample size near zero. Combined with
the costs above, this gives a cost per experimental-label-equivalent---the
quantity that actually decides whether a simulation campaign is worth running. The
FEP curve is not monotonic, so the figure is coarse, and a larger, cleaner source
pool would sharpen it; the computation and its inputs are provided with the
analysis code.

A large and diverse $\Delta\Delta G$ dataset would also let the central
quantitative question be tested directly. Whether held-out Tm error falls
predictably as the source pool grows---the scaling behavior that makes simulation
databases worth building in materials informatics
\citep{Minami2025Scaling}---could not be settled here, because our pool was small
and the label-count curve was not monotonic. Establishing that relationship would
turn the qualitative rule, that more physically relevant data helps, into a
quantitative guide for how much such data to collect.

\subsection*{Limitations}

Several limitations bound the interpretation. The experimental Tm training set is
small, so the conclusions should be retested on other nanobody collections and
protein families. The source-label comparison covers only the labels available
here; other free-energy protocols, stability predictors, or trajectory
descriptors may transfer differently. The FEP curve supports a beneficial trend
at the largest setting but does not establish a clean scaling exponent. No new
experimental measurements were made to validate high-stability candidates. And
because raw trajectories may be too large to deposit in full, reproducibility will
rely on processed data, scripts, and a clearly reported simulation protocol.

\subsection*{Outlook}

For low-data experimental protein-property prediction, simulation labels are best
treated as design choices rather than generic data augmentation. The question is
not whether simulation can produce many labels, but whether those labels encode
information that improves the target task under target-centered validation. Here,
mutation free energies did that for nanobody Tm. The general recipe follows: use
experimental data to define the target phenotype, use simulation to annotate
physically meaningful perturbations around it, and judge the combined model only
on held-out experimental data. That is a practical route from simulation-assisted
prediction toward closed-loop protein engineering.

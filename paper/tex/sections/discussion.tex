\section*{Discussion}

\subsection*{Source-label transfer is selective}

This study asked how simulation-derived labels should be used when the
experimental target is scarce and the simulated quantity is related to, but not
identical with, that target. Using nanobody Tm prediction as a case study, we
found that FEP mutation free-energy labels produced a small but consistent
improvement in held-out experimental Tm prediction. The same improvement was not
reproduced by the MD-derived Q-value descriptor tested here, nor was it
explained by using a larger ESM2 encoder. These findings support a selective
view of Sim2Real learning for biomolecules: simulation data are useful when the
source label encodes physical information that transfers to the experimental
phenotype under target-centered validation.

\subsection*{Mutation free-energy labels connect relative and absolute stability}

The helpful source label did not measure Tm itself. FEP estimates mutation
free-energy changes, whereas Tm is an experimental phenotype reflecting the
temperature at which folded and unfolded states are balanced under a particular
assay condition. These are not interchangeable quantities. Nevertheless, both
describe the same underlying stability landscape from different perspectives.
Sparse Tm measurements anchor absolute thermal-stability values for observed
sequences, while mutation free-energy labels provide local directions in
sequence space. The FEP improvement therefore suggests that source labels can
help even when they are not direct surrogates for the target, provided that they
describe a physically relevant perturbation of the target property.

\subsection*{Scaling behavior depends on the source label}

These results extend the materials-informatics Sim2Real argument to a more
heterogeneous biomolecular setting. In materials applications, transfer learning
often links computational and experimental estimates of related material
properties, and scaling behavior can quantify the downstream value of a growing
computational database \citep{Minami2025Scaling}. In the present protein case,
the most useful computational labels were not additional Tm measurements but
mutation free energies. The label-count sweep for FEP was consistent with a
benefit at the largest tested setting, but it was not smooth enough to claim a
universal biomolecular scaling law. The stronger conclusion is source
dependence: simulation-derived data should be evaluated by target-task
performance, and source labels must be chosen for transferable physical content
rather than availability alone.

\subsection*{Model-size controls}

The encoder controls sharpen this conclusion. Prior work on PLMs has shown that
fine-tuning can be critical for downstream protein-property prediction
\citep{Schmirler2024FineTuning}, and related nanobody Tm analyses suggest that
larger pretrained ESM2 models do not automatically produce better Tm predictors.
Our own size controls reached the same practical conclusion: 35M and 650M ESM2
encoders did not outperform the 8M encoder in either Tm-only or FEP-assisted
settings. The best model used the 8M encoder with FEP source labels. Thus, in
this dataset, increasing language-model capacity did not substitute for source
labels carrying information about sequence-dependent stability changes.

\subsection*{Encoder adaptation and descriptor choice}

The frozen-encoder experiment further indicates how the source-label signal is
used. FEP labels improved performance even when the encoder was fixed, showing
that part of the signal can be captured by the existing sequence
representation. However, the best FEP model required updating the encoder. This
suggests that FEP labels help most when they are allowed to reshape the
representation toward stability-relevant sequence directions while still being
selected by experimental Tm performance. In practical terms, the result argues
for controlled encoder adaptation rather than either fully fixed feature
extraction or unconstrained source-task optimization.

The MD-derived Q-value result should be interpreted with the same specificity.
It should not be read as evidence that MD-derived descriptors are generally
unhelpful. Rather, this particular high-temperature native-contact summary did
not transfer to Tm prediction under the present model-selection protocol. The
descriptor may still capture aspects of structural durability, but it compresses
each trajectory to a single native-contact persistence statistic and may miss
mutation-specific energetic effects that determine how sequence changes alter
the stability landscape. A simulation campaign optimized for target transfer
should therefore choose descriptors whose physical meaning is expected to
transfer to the experimental endpoint, rather than maximizing label count alone.

\subsection*{Physics-scored sequence proposals as a future direction}

The Rosetta-scored proposal-set result is best viewed as a feasibility test for
future design workflows. Rosetta scores assigned to ESM2-proposed variants
improved Tm prediction relative to Tm-only learning, but the direct comparison
with random variants remained exploratory because its confidence interval
crossed zero. We therefore do not interpret this result as evidence that the
current proposal model has already optimized nanobody thermostability. The
useful point is more limited: sequences proposed by a protein language model can
be annotated by a physics-based score and then used as supervised source data
for a Tm predictor.

That supervised-scoring step is the only part of a design loop tested here. In a
future closed loop, a sequence generator could propose variants, physics-based
calculations could annotate them, the Tm predictor could update its estimate of
experimental stability, and the resulting score could be used as a reward or
selection signal for the next round of proposals. Such a workflow would connect
the present transfer-learning result to active learning or reinforcement
learning, but those closed-loop steps and the experimental validation of newly
proposed high-Tm nanobodies remain outside the scope of this paper.

\subsection*{Limitations}

Several limitations should guide interpretation. First, the experimental Tm
training set is small, and the conclusions should be tested on additional
nanobody collections and other protein families. Second, the source-label
comparison is limited to the computational labels available here. Other
free-energy protocols, stability predictors, trajectory summaries, or
structure-based descriptors may transfer differently. Third, the FEP curve does
not establish a clean power-law scaling relation; it supports a beneficial trend
at the largest tested setting, but not a universal scaling exponent. Fourth, no
new experimental measurements were performed to validate high-stability
candidates. Finally, raw simulation trajectories may be too large to deposit
fully, so reproducibility will depend on processed data, scripts, and clear
reporting of the simulation protocol.

\subsection*{Outlook}

For low-data experimental protein-property prediction, simulation labels should
be treated as design choices rather than as generic data augmentation. The
relevant question is not whether simulations can produce many labels, but
whether those labels encode information that improves the target task under a
target-centered validation protocol. In this study, mutation free-energy labels
provided that information for nanobody Tm prediction. The broader strategy is
therefore to use experimental data to define the target phenotype, use
simulation to annotate physically meaningful perturbations around that
phenotype, and evaluate the combined model only on held-out experimental data.
This provides a practical path from simulation-assisted prediction toward future
closed-loop protein engineering.

\begin{abstract}
Nanobody development often depends on thermal stability, but experimental
melting-temperature (Tm) measurements remain scarce. Simulations and
structure-based calculations can annotate many sequence variants, yet their
labels usually describe related physical quantities rather than Tm itself. We
asked whether such computational labels can improve low-data nanobody Tm
prediction when every model choice is anchored to experimental Tm validation.
Using a public nanobody Tm benchmark, we trained transfer-learning models that
combined Tm prediction with one computational source task at a time. The source
labels covered mutation free energies, structure-based scores, protein-language
model proposal sets scored by Rosetta, and molecular-dynamics trajectory
summaries. Alchemical free-energy perturbation (FEP) gave the clearest benefit:
held-out Tm mean absolute error decreased from 6.61 to 6.26$^\circ$C relative
to training only on experimental Tm labels, with a paired improvement of
0.35$^\circ$C (90\% confidence interval, 0.24--0.46$^\circ$C). The gain was not
reproduced by increasing the ESM2 encoder size or by using the MD-derived
Q-value. Rosetta-scored proposal sets were feasible as supervised source labels,
but the direct advantage of ESM2-proposed variants over random variants remained
exploratory.
Thus, computational labels can improve nanobody Tm prediction when their
physical meaning transfers to the experimental endpoint; mutation free energies
complement sparse absolute Tm measurements.
\end{abstract}

\begin{abstract}
Thermal stability limits how far a nanobody can be engineered, yet experimental
melting-temperature (Tm) measurements are scarce. Mutation free energies and
related stability quantities can be computed, or now measured, at far larger
scale, yet they are usually treated as a prediction problem separate from Tm. A
relative, per-mutation free-energy change is not an absolute melting temperature,
and there is no a priori reason that learning one should help predict the other.
We tested whether such labels, used as auxiliary tasks, transfer to low-data Tm
prediction when models are selected only against experimental Tm. On a public
benchmark of 57 training, 114 validation, and 396 held-out test sequences, we
trained multi-task models in which a Tm predictor shared a
protein-language-model representation with one computational source task at a
time, ranging from mutation free energies and structure-based mutation scores to
Rosetta scores for language-model-proposed variants and molecular-dynamics (MD)
trajectory summaries. Mutation free energies from alchemical free-energy
perturbation (FEP) transferred best, lowering held-out Tm mean absolute error
from 6.61 to 6.26$^\circ$C, a consistent paired gain of 0.35$^\circ$C (90\%
confidence interval, 0.24--0.46$^\circ$C). The transfer was selective rather than
generic. An MD-derived Q-value did not help at all, and enlarging the ESM2
encoder did not reproduce the FEP gain, so the benefit tracks the physical
relatedness of the source label rather than added data or capacity.
Rosetta-scored proposal sets also worked as source labels, but their edge over
random variants stayed within noise. Free-energy labels can therefore supply
transferable supervision for the scarce Tm phenotype when their physical meaning
carries over to it.
\end{abstract}

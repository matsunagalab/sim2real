\begin{abstract}
Experimental thermal-stability measurements for nanobodies are scarce, whereas
simulation and physics-based calculations can produce many labels for related
sequence perturbations. The practical question is how to use those labels when
they do not measure the experimental endpoint itself. Here we study this problem
for nanobody melting-temperature (Tm) prediction using NbBench as the
experimental target dataset. We trained multi-task transfer models in which an
ESM2 sequence encoder is shared by the Tm target task and computational source
tasks, but all checkpoint selection, model selection, and final evaluation remain
anchored to experimental Tm data. The source tasks included FEP-derived mutation
free energies, Rosetta and ThermoMPNN mutation-effect scores, Rosetta scores on
generated and random variants, and an MD-derived contact-persistence label. FEP
labels gave the clearest improvement: held-out Tm mean absolute error decreased
from 6.61 to 6.26$^\circ$C relative to Tm-only training, with a paired
improvement of 0.35$^\circ$C (90\% confidence interval, 0.24--0.46$^\circ$C).
The effect was not explained by simply using a larger ESM2 encoder, and it was
not reproduced by the MD-derived contact-persistence label. Rosetta-scored
generated variants gave a weaker but positive signal. These results show that
simulation labels are useful for Tm prediction only when their physical content
transfers to the experimental phenotype. In this case, mutation free energies
appear to complement sparse absolute Tm measurements by providing local
stability-change information around sequence variants.
\end{abstract}

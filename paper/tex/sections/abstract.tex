\begin{abstract}
Nanobodies are single-domain antibody fragments whose practical development
often depends on thermal stability. A common experimental readout is the melting
temperature (Tm), the temperature at which a protein loses half of its folded
population under a given assay condition. Experimental Tm measurements are
scarce for nanobodies, whereas molecular simulations and structure-based
calculations can produce many related labels for mutated or designed sequences.
The practical problem is that these computational labels are usually not Tm
measurements. We therefore asked how such labels should be used for
experimental Tm prediction when they measure related but different physical
quantities. Using a public nanobody Tm benchmark, we trained transfer-learning
models in which a pretrained protein sequence model learned the experimental Tm
task together with one computational prediction task at a time. To keep the
comparison experimental, checkpoint selection, model selection, and final
evaluation were all based only on experimental Tm validation or test examples.
We compared mutation free-energy changes from alchemical free-energy
perturbation (FEP) calculations, structure-based mutation scores, learned
stability-change scores, physics scores on generated and random sequence
variants, and a molecular-dynamics trajectory summary that measures retained
native contacts. The FEP mutation free-energy labels gave the clearest
improvement: held-out Tm mean absolute error decreased from 6.61 to
6.26$^\circ$C relative to training on Tm labels alone, with a paired improvement
of 0.35$^\circ$C (90\% confidence interval, 0.24--0.46$^\circ$C). The
improvement was not explained by using a larger protein sequence model and was
not reproduced by the contact-persistence trajectory summary. Structure-scored
generated variants gave a weaker but positive signal. These results show that
simulation data can improve low-data Tm prediction, but only when the computed
label carries physical information that transfers to the experimental endpoint.
In this case, sparse absolute Tm measurements and relative mutation
free-energy changes provide complementary views of nanobody stability.
\end{abstract}

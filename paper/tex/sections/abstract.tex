\begin{abstract}
Experimental measurements of nanobody melting temperature (Tm) are scarce, which
limits machine-learning models of thermal stability. Simulations can provide more
training labels, but these labels describe properties related to Tm rather than Tm
itself. We asked how the choice of simulated variants and measured quantity affects
their usefulness. We trained multi-task models that used a shared ESM2 sequence
model for experimental Tm and one computed label at a time. We used 57 sequences
for training, 114 for validation, and 396 for held-out testing. Each label and
encoder setting was tuned separately using only the Tm validation set, then
compared on the same test proteins. The choice of simulated variants mattered. An
MD native-contact label reduced frozen-encoder Tm error by 0.20$^\circ$C when
computed for local mutation scans on two fixed structures, compared with
0.05$^\circ$C for a mixed set of sequences. The measured quantity also mattered.
Free-energy perturbation (FEP) labels improved both frozen and fine-tuned encoders,
reducing the fine-tuned mean absolute error from 6.55$^\circ$C to 6.40$^\circ$C.
Native-contact labels helped only the frozen encoder, while Rosetta and ThermoMPNN
scores gave little or no improvement. These results show that simulation data for
experimental prediction should be built from carefully chosen variants and from
quantities that are closely related to the experimental property.
\end{abstract}

\vspace{0.5em}
\noindent\textbf{Significance}\\
Simulations can provide more protein-stability labels than experiments, but those
labels do not always improve prediction of an experimental property. For nanobody
Tm prediction, two choices mattered: which variants were simulated and which
quantity was calculated. Native-contact labels helped more when computed for
local mutation scans than for a mixed set of sequences. Free-energy labels helped
both frozen and fine-tuned sequence models, whereas native-contact labels helped
only the frozen model. More simulation is therefore not enough; the variants and
the calculated quantity must be chosen carefully.

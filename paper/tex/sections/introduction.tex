\section*{Introduction}

Machine learning can now screen enormous molecular and materials spaces before
any wet-lab test, but it is only as good as the property labels it learns from.
In materials informatics, this label bottleneck motivated a simulation-to-real
(Sim2Real) strategy: train on large computational databases generated by
first-principles calculations, quantum chemistry, or molecular dynamics, and
transfer the resulting model to a smaller experimental target set
\citep{Yamada2019Predicting,Aoki2023Multitask,Minami2025Scaling}. For polymers
and inorganic materials, the transferred error can fall predictably as the
computational database grows, which gives a concrete way to judge whether more
simulation is worth generating \citep{Minami2025Scaling}. The premise matters
because simulation data are not useful by default: model assumptions, finite
sampling, and systematic domain gaps separate any calculation from the
measurement it stands in for.

Protein engineering has the same bottleneck with an extra complication.
Experimental measurements are expensive and sparse, while simulations and
physics-based calculations can label many designed or mutated sequences. But the
simulated quantity is usually not the experimental target, and is often handled
as its own prediction problem. A protein's melting temperature (Tm)---the
absolute temperature at which half of the molecules are unfolded under a given
assay---is one such target. What can be computed or measured at scale is usually
something adjacent: a mutation's free-energy change ($\Delta\Delta G$), a
structure-based stability score, or a summary of a molecular-dynamics trajectory.
A quantity such as $\Delta\Delta G$ is relative and per-mutation, evaluated on its
own datasets, and there is no a priori reason that supervising a model with it
should improve an absolute Tm measured on different sequences. The question is
therefore not whether simulation data can be added to training, but which
simulated quantities actually transfer to the experimental phenotype.

We study this question in nanobodies. These single-domain VHH antibodies are
appealing scaffolds---small, modular, and robust binders
\citep{Muyldermans2013Nanobodies,Alexander2024Discovery}---but their use in
expression, storage, and formulation hinges on thermal stability
\citep{Kunz2018-qt,Mezzasalma2007-ci,Kumar2000-te}, and measuring or simulating
that stability directly is costly
\citep{Bekker2019Thermal,Higashida2021-lt,Mohseni2019-lx,Tomimoto2023-ww}.
Sequence-based predictors built on protein language models (PLMs) are an
attractive shortcut: models such as ESM2 learn general-purpose representations
from large sequence corpora \citep{Rives2021Biological,Lin2023Evolutionary,Hayes2025-pd}, and
several methods now read Tm out of such representations
\citep{Blaabjerg2023Rapid,Li2023DeepTM,Alvarez2024TEMPRO,Savojardo2024-tv,Ramon2025-oy,Mao2026-yf}.
High-throughput
assays are reshaping the data landscape as well: Tsuboyama et al. used
cDNA-display proteolysis to measure folding stability for roughly 776{,}000
natural and designed domains \citep{Tsuboyama2023MegaScale}. That scale is
remarkable, but it does not yet reach nanobodies. The curated domains span
40--72 residues, whereas VHH domains are considerably longer, and comparable
high-throughput $\Delta G$ data for them do not exist; a model fine-tuned on the
mega-scale set also transferred less well to 177--501-residue proteins than to
small domains \citep{Chu2024EsmTherm}. Large stability resources are powerful,
then, but target-specific supervision and careful validation remain essential
for nanobody Tm prediction
\citep{ValdesTresanco2023NbThermo,Zhang2025NbBench,Schmirler2024FineTuning}.

Here we test whether simulation-derived labels can supply that supervision when
experimental Tm is scarce. We treat Tm prediction as the target task and each
computational label as a source task that shapes a shared sequence
representation, with ESM2 as the encoder \citep{Lin2023Evolutionary}. The source
labels span several kinds of information: mutation free energies from alchemical
free-energy perturbation (FEP), Rosetta mutation scores, ThermoMPNN stability
scores, Rosetta scores on variants proposed by ESM2 or drawn at random, and an
MD-derived Q-value that tracks native-contact persistence. To keep the
comparison honest, every model setting is chosen on an experimental validation
set and every final number comes from the same held-out experimental Tm test
set, using paired error comparisons. This prevents a model that excels at a
computational task from being mistaken for a better Tm predictor.

The benefit turns out to be source-dependent, and it crosses a task boundary that
is usually kept separate. Mutation free energies from FEP---a relative,
per-mutation quantity---give the clearest improvement in absolute Tm prediction
over training on Tm alone, whereas the MD-derived Q-value, though equally
simulation-derived, gives none under the same protocol. Rosetta-scored ESM2
proposals give a weaker, exploratory signal that points toward future
generator--physics--predictor design loops without demonstrating closed-loop
optimization. Together these results carry the materials-informatics Sim2Real
argument into a setting where the source and target labels differ in physical
meaning: useful simulation data are not merely abundant, they encode a quantity
that transfers to the experimental property of interest.

\section{Introduction}

Nanobodies are single-domain VHH antibody fragments used as small, modular
binding proteins
\citep{Muyldermans2013Nanobodies,Alexander2024Discovery}. Their thermal
stability affects how reliably they can be expressed, purified, and stored
\citep{Kunz2018-qt,Mezzasalma2007-ci,Kumar2000-te}, yet both direct measurements
and detailed stability simulations remain costly
\citep{Bekker2019Thermal,Higashida2021-lt,Mohseni2019-lx,Tomimoto2023-ww}.
Melting temperature (Tm) is a common experimental measure of thermal stability,
but only a limited number of nanobody Tm values are available for model
training.

Protein language models such as ESM2 learn sequence features from large protein
databases \citep{Rives2021Biological,Lin2023Evolutionary,Hayes2025-pd}, and
several methods use these features to predict Tm
\citep{Blaabjerg2023Rapid,Li2023DeepTM,Alvarez2024TEMPRO,
Savojardo2024-tv,Ramon2025-oy,Mao2026-yf}. For family-specific prediction,
measured Tm values are still needed for training and evaluation. Large
experimental stability sets exist for other protein classes: Tsuboyama et al.,
for example, reported on the order of $10^5$ high-quality folding-stability
measurements for single mutants and selected double mutants of several hundred
natural and designed protein domains \citep{Tsuboyama2023MegaScale}. Those
domains are 40--72 residues long and differ from VHH domains in both sequence
and measurement type, and a model fine-tuned on these short domains was less
accurate on longer proteins \citep{Chu2024EsmTherm}. Nanobody-specific data and
validation therefore remain necessary
\citep{ValdesTresanco2023NbThermo,Zhang2025NbBench,
Schmirler2024FineTuning}.

Computed properties offer another way to supplement scarce experimental data.
Combining a large in silico data set with a smaller experimental one is often
called simulation-to-real (Sim2Real) learning, and this approach has improved
materials-property prediction in several settings
\citep{Yamada2019Predicting,Aoki2023Multitask,Minami2025Scaling}. More computed
examples are not always a better experimental predictor, however, because a
calculation may be inaccurate for the systems of interest, may cover systems
unrelated to the target, or may report a quantity that is only weakly connected
to the experiment.

The last point is the acute one for protein stability, because what a
calculation returns is rarely what an experiment measures. Tm is the midpoint of
a thermal-unfolding transition under specified assay conditions. Stability
calculations instead usually report the change in folding free energy caused by
a mutation ($\Delta\Delta G$): free-energy perturbation (FEP) estimates
$\Delta\Delta G$ from alchemical simulations \citep{Bennett1976BAR}, Rosetta
estimates it from an empirical energy function
\citep{Kellogg2011RosettaDDG,Alford2017Rosetta}, and learned models such as
ThermoMPNN predict it from structure \citep{Dieckhaus2024ThermoMPNN}. Molecular
dynamics (MD) reports a different kind of quantity altogether, such as the
fraction of native contacts retained during a high-temperature trajectory
\citep{Best2013NativeContacts}. Each of these concerns protein stability, but
none of them is Tm, so their value as auxiliary data for Tm prediction cannot be
assumed.

Two decisions on study design therefore precede any Sim2Real model training, and
both must be made before the simulations begin. The first is which sequence
variants to compute. A heterogeneous set drawn from many structures may carry
unwanted differences, such as sequence length, whereas a mutation scan on one or
two fixed structures gives a narrower and more controlled set of sequence
changes. The second is which quantity to compute for those variants, since
$\Delta\Delta G$ from FEP, a Rosetta score, and an MD native-contact value need
not carry the same information about Tm. Which of these decisions governs
whether computed stability data transfer to a measured Tm, and does computing
more examples help on its own? This is the question we address here.

To address this question, we evaluated several computed quantities in the same
low-data Tm task: mutation free energies from FEP, Rosetta mutation scores,
ThermoMPNN scores, Rosetta scores for ESM2-proposed or random variants, and MD
native-contact values. For MD, we examined a heterogeneous structure panel and
local mutation scans on the same 1MEL and 4IDL structures used for FEP. Both MD
data sets used the same force field, water model, simulation engine, and 400~K
protocol, and both recorded the same native-contact quantity; they differed in
the variants covered and in the contact selection and averaging window used to
compute it. We therefore compare the two MD data sets as they were built. For
each data source, the model was trained on Tm and one computed quantity using
ESM2 sequence representations (Fig.~\ref{fig:learning_framework}). Model
settings were selected using only experimental Tm validation data. The selected
models were then compared using paired absolute errors for the same 396 test
sequences, which were used neither for training nor for model selection. We use
``transfer'' to mean a reduction in test Tm error after adding computed data.

We found that both design decisions, and not the amount of computed data,
determined whether transfer occurred. Among the computed quantities, FEP
$\Delta\Delta G$ gave the lowest test error with both encoder settings, and MD
native-contact labels reduced error with the frozen encoder, whereas Rosetta and
ThermoMPNN labels gave little or no improvement. Among the two ways of choosing
variants, the local mutation scans gave a larger frozen-encoder gain than the
heterogeneous panel. The number of computed values alone did not predict
transfer performance: performance instead differed among data sets that varied
in their selected sequences and computed quantities. Because these choices are
fixed once the simulations are run, and the simulations are the expensive step,
this finding suggests spending a computational budget on a small, targeted
mutation scan of a quantity close to the measured property rather than on a
larger but loosely related data set. That reasoning is not specific to nanobody
Tm, and we expect it to apply to other Sim2Real settings in which the computed
and measured quantities differ.

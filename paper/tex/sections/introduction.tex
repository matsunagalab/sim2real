\section{Introduction}

Nanobodies are single-domain VHH antibody fragments used as small, modular
binding proteins
\citep{Muyldermans2013Nanobodies,Alexander2024Discovery}. Their thermal
stability affects expression, storage, and formulation
\citep{Kunz2018-qt,Mezzasalma2007-ci,Kumar2000-te}, yet both direct measurements
and detailed stability simulations remain costly
\citep{Bekker2019Thermal,Higashida2021-lt,Mohseni2019-lx,Tomimoto2023-ww}.
Melting temperature (Tm) is a common experimental measure of thermal stability,
but only a limited number of nanobody Tm values are available for model
training.

Protein language models such as ESM2 learn sequence features from large protein
databases \citep{Rives2021Biological,Lin2023Evolutionary,Hayes2025-pd}, and
several methods use these features to predict Tm
\citep{Blaabjerg2023Rapid,Li2023DeepTM,Alvarez2024TEMPRO,
Savojardo2024-tv,Ramon2025-oy,Mao2026-yf}. For family-specific prediction,
measured Tm values are still needed for training and evaluation.
Tsuboyama et al., for example, reported approximately 776{,}000 high-quality
folding-stability measurements for single mutants and selected double mutants
of 331 natural and 148 designed protein domains
\citep{Tsuboyama2023MegaScale}. The domains are 40--72 residues long and differ
from VHH domains in both sequence and measurement type. A model fine-tuned on
these short domains was substantially less accurate on longer proteins
\citep{Chu2024EsmTherm}. Nanobody-specific data and validation remain necessary
\citep{ValdesTresanco2023NbThermo,Zhang2025NbBench,
Schmirler2024FineTuning}.

Calculated properties offer another way to supplement scarce experimental
data. Combining a large computed data set with a smaller experimental one is
often called simulation-to-real (Sim2Real) learning. This approach has improved
materials-property prediction in several settings
\citep{Yamada2019Predicting,Aoki2023Multitask,Minami2025Scaling}. More computed
examples do not guarantee a better experimental predictor, however. A
calculation may be inaccurate, cover systems unrelated to the target, or report
a quantity with only a weak connection to the experiment.

This distinction is especially important for protein stability. Tm is the midpoint
of a thermal-unfolding transition under specified assay conditions. A mutation
free-energy change ($\Delta\Delta G$) instead describes how a sequence change
alters folding free energy. Rosetta and learned stability models produce scores
on their own scales, while molecular dynamics (MD) can be used to calculate
structural quantities such as the fraction of native contacts retained during a
trajectory. All concern protein stability, but none of the calculated
quantities is Tm. Their value as auxiliary data for Tm prediction is therefore
uncertain.

Using calculated stability data requires two decisions before model training.
The first is which sequence variants to include. A heterogeneous set may contain
unwanted differences, such as sequence length, whereas a mutation scan on one
or two fixed structures gives a narrower set of sequence changes. The second is
which quantity to record. Mutation free energy, a Rosetta score, and an MD
native-contact value need not carry the same information about Tm. The value of
the added data may depend on both choices.

We evaluated several calculated quantities in the same low-data Tm task:
mutation free energies from free-energy perturbation (FEP), Rosetta mutation
scores, ThermoMPNN scores, Rosetta scores for ESM2-proposed or random variants,
and MD native-contact values. For MD, we examined a heterogeneous structure
panel and local mutation scans on the same 1MEL and 4IDL structures used for
FEP. Both studies used 400~K MD and native-contact values, but they differed in
contact selection, trajectory averaging, and other details. We therefore
compare the two MD studies as implemented and do not attribute their difference
to sequence choice alone.

Each model was trained on Tm and one calculated quantity using ESM2 sequence
representations (Fig.~\ref{fig:learning_framework}). Model settings were
selected using only experimental Tm validation data. The selected models were
then compared using paired absolute errors for the same 396 test sequences,
which were not used for training or model selection. We use ``transfer'' to mean
a reduction in test Tm error after adding calculated data.

The local mutation-scan MD study gave a larger frozen-encoder gain than the
heterogeneous-panel study. Among the calculated quantities, FEP gave the lowest
test MAE with both encoder settings, whereas the 95\% interval for the
change in MAE from MD native-contact labels excluded zero only with the frozen
encoder. Across these comparisons, the number of calculated values alone did
not predict transfer performance. Performance
differed among data sets that varied in their selected sequences, calculated
quantities, and other details.
